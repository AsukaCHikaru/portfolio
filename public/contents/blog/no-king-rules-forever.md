On November 2nd, 2018, hundreds of people sat in a large hall in Anaheim, California. Before them was a man standing on stage, microphone attached to his right cheek. His voice, along with excitement, lingered in the air. He had just announced _Diablo: Immortal_, the latest entry in the legendary game franchise _Diablo_. Two cinematic trailers were played, one photo-realistic, choreographed like a movie but revealed nothing about the game, the other showcasing the gameplay. At the start and the end of the gameplay trailer was a smartphone in landscape, all footage on its screen; of five of the feature text cards two mentioned the word "mobile". Following the announcement panel was a Q&A session. An attendee asked: "[...] Is there any plans to make this playable on PC? Or is this strictly mobile forever?" The speaker responded promptly. "Ahh are there any..." he stuttered, "Oh yeah this...the current plan is to be on mobile, both Android and iOS. We don't have any plans at the moment to do a PC." He finished with a smile. Boos immediately followed. The speaker looked around and found that they came from every corner of the hall. Intending to de-escalate the apparent anger with a laugh, maybe, he jokingly responded to the crowd: "Do you guys not have phones?" This phrase is infamous among the gaming community; entries with the same name can be found in many urban dictionaries, in them is recorded the incident, remembered as a signature moment of a company that lost connection with its customers, the fall from grace of the once beloved video game developer _Blizzard_. Their new game announcements weren't always disastrous like this. Quite the opposite, actually. In 2007 in Seoul, the reveal of _StarCraft II: Wings of Liberty_ was welcomed with thunderous cheering, applause, and immense excitement; instead of a joke that showed the detachment from their audience, SC2's announcement trailer matched the event location as well as the biggest SC community, as both the animation and the voice acting were in Korean, even the lips were synched. The three-year wait until launch further escalated the anticipation: beta keys were sold for hundreds of dollars on auction sites; a leaked client from the closed beta was shared through peer-to-peer downloads for those who wanted a glimpse. On launch day, launch parties were held all over the world; more than a million of copies sold in the first day. At the time, all the Blizzard games were phenomenal, even industry-defining: in 1998, _StarCraft_ became the synonym for esports, and a whole industry in South Korea; in 2000, _Diablo II_ defined an isometric loot-grinding ARPG subgenre named after it, later called "diablo-like"; in 2002, _WarCraft III_, with its powerful map editor and hero unit-oriented gameplay, inspired many ideas which later became dedicated, world-famous games; in 2004, _World of Warcraft_ changed MMORPG forever. All of this was on PC. Blizzard, with one masterpiece after another for more than a decade, naturally built a loyal, enthusiastic fanbase, entirely on PC gaming.

Before gaming websites were a thing, my only source of PC gaming information was magazines. Upcoming titles’ news, monthly sales rankings, and most importantly, playthrough guides. The editors picked up the most anticipated new releases, beat them before the deadline, and wrote up detailed playthrough notes, providing information necessary for beating the game: tips, maps, step by step walkthrough guides. For narrative-driven games there would also be a story summary alongside the walkthrough. Given that the number of games my parents bought me was a fraction of the number I read, the guides were an essential block of my PC gaming entertainment. By the time I started reading them StarCraft had already passed its prime, but I made it to Diablo II. The editors’ words were filled with excitement and admiration for the game, so much that, although I didn’t know what the game was about, it was probably a very anticipated one. I read the guides over and over, learning the names _Deckard Cain_, _Tal Rasha_ and _The Gidbinn_ before eventually asking my mother to buy me the game. The depth of the game was beyond everything I had ever played as well as what I could comprehend at the time, the most I could do was struggling through normal difficulty with absolutely no idea about the concept of endgame, until my classmate enlightened me with Baal and cow level runs. Still, I played without any plan, blindly copying skill orders while knowing nothing about itemization. That didn't stop me from enjoying reading D2 articles in the magazines regardless; while I was too casual to access most of the content referred to, reading all the magic find strategies, build-craftings and cube recipes was still as fun as it could be for me. They helped me to, despite only being able to scratch the surface in my playthrough, appreciate the depth and grow a basic literacy about the game, so when I heard people talking about _Enigma_, _Titan's Revenge_ or _pindle runs_, I understood them and felt like part of the community. The name "Blizzard" and the fact they created this amazing game did not register yet. One time my buddy referred to a weapon called _渣砲_ (zhapao). That’s a weird name for a weapon, I thought. “What’s a Zhapao?” “Blizzard cannon[^1].” He showed his inventory to me. I associated the name with the crossbow he was using but still didn’t get the name. The weapon's in-game name not being a comprehensible noun didn’t help either. It turned out the Traditional Chinese translator didn’t realize the name _Buriza-Do Kyanon_ was the Japanese romanization of _Blizzard Cannon_ and translated the name with a string of similarly pronounced Chinese words. The concept of Blizzard the developer entered my vision with WoW, an MMORPG which requires constant updates and in-game moderation, that is, more developer presence. While from time to time people complained about the balances, overall Blizzard’s reputation was very good. They were the developer who wouldn’t rush a launch date for the quarterly numbers but only release “when it’s done”. A delayed release was, nevertheless, a bummer because their new releases were always extremely anticipated, but it also meant a more polished product, and as there was not a shortage of publishers who prioritized profit over the players, Blizzard’s stance was cherished. Every April Fools’ Day they would make a high-effort joke as if it was the biggest event of the year, while at other times of the year silly easter eggs were added to the games as well. _Wirt_, a peg-legged boy from Diablo, reappears in Diablo II, cold, dead. When he is clicked his wooden leg rolls out as a single-hand weapon. A dark reminder of what happened in the prequel. The leg, however, can be used as the ingredient to open a portal to the secret cow level, a small farm where poleaxe-wielding bipedal bovines roam. It's a breaking-the-fourth-wall joke that shouldn't exist in the Diablo world yet at the same time absolutely legit endgame content, as they drop decent experience and loot. On April Fools' Day 2002 they "announced" _pandaren_, a humanoid intelligent panda, as the fifth playable race of WarCraft III, and while that didn't happen in the game, traces of the joke can still be found in things such as a hidden artwork or neutral creeps. A year later in the expansion they added a real, playable neutral hero, _The Pandaren Brewmaster_. In 2012, ten years after the original joke, they made a whole WoW expansion–years of development time, hundreds hours' worth of content–entirely built around pandaren. Famous player in the community such as _Leeroy Jenkins_ or _The Red Shirt Guy_ were celebrated and immortalized as NPCs in the game.

I first played WoW vanilla–the nickname of the original version–in the Taiwanese beta, in 2005. At the time I had heard people playing on the US server talking about how good the game was and how it was different from all the Korean soulless repetitive farming MMOs. The fanaticism made me a bit skeptical, but since trying new MMOs while they were in beta was a common practice, plus I liked WarCraft III, playing it was a natural decision. It turned out it wasn’t like anything I had ever played. I saw on a small hill a lonely tower watching over a land supposed to be abundant but instead covered with roaming bandits; tumbleweeds rolled over abandoned farms as the wind blew, sky dyed yellow by dust. I walked on a pass in a corridor of ash trees, watching for and avoiding predators lurk in the woods; the trees as high as the sky, their leafs blocking the sunshine, the forest in dim blue. I visited a small town alongside a peaceful lake surrounded by every kind of threat, from local burglars and aggressive wildlife to hostile factions, tension brewed in the air, children played next to a bridge torn down for its metal used in the previous war, scars still exposed under the sun. I had to rely on nothing but my feet, running for 10, 20 minutes just to talk to a certain NPC or to find a certain location, seeing the world during the journey. In today's terms there was nothing but friction, but the friction immersed me into the world, and it was the most beautiful part of the game; it was the _world_ of Warcraft, after all. On top of it were the dungeons and the adventures. Deep in _The Deadmines_ was more than a bunch of bandits hiding in a cave; five players, having no idea what was before them, fought their way ahead, one pack of mobs at a time. At the end of the tunnels was an underground factory, molten steel dripping in a huge furnace next to us as we descended, goblin mechanics yelling for reinforcement; our provisions low yet bags full of loot, weapons and armor nearly broken from countless deaths. Finally after a wipe we decided to teleport back to town, regroup, then embark again. Nobody left the party. The run took the entire night. I don't remember what equipment upgrades I got from that run; odds are that I had none. What remained was the run itself. So were the Gnomeregan run and the Blackfathom Deeps run. The storytelling, the excitement for the unknown ahead, and the companionship, all created a world that only existed at that time. I didn’t know it, and I didn’t appreciate it enough. Two months later, after a bad mark in the new school year, I mailed a friend I met in the game, giving him all my items before I uninstalled the game.

As I also mentioned in [my previous post](https://asukawang.com/blog/hesitation-is-defeat), there was an anecdote about vanilla WoW development that Blizzard spent a month polishing the _Fireball_ ability. I first heard about the story years ago, then wanted to find the details when I started writing the post. Claude could never find the source (a friend's GPT-5.6 Sol max found it in one try) but instead told me about a book by former WoW dungeon designer John Staats: _The WoW Diary: A Journal of Computer Game Development_, a memoir of vanilla WoW development. I read the entire book. While what I was looking for wasn't in the book, it was nonetheless a very worthwhile read, as the story showed me a past I didn't know, and it brought me a mix of amazement and melancholy. "The Team 2 area was a dump, decorated like someone’s basement. [...] The closest thing to a kitchen was a tiny microwave next to a sink filled with dirty dishes. Food stains, blackened with age, had been ground into the carpet. [...] The walls were covered with dog-eared posters, and every desk and shelf was laden with dusty statues and action figures. People walked around wearing shorts and flip-flops.” If I had to imagine a _good_ game developer's office–if I had to imagine *Blizzard's office*–in the late 90s to early 00s, this is probably what I would come up with. There would be pizza boxes, comic books, movie and game posters, in addition to laughter and enthusiastic conversations regarding either _Dungeons and Dragons_ or software engineering. People would work overtime, play some _Unreal Tournament_, then get back to work more late hours; it would be by no means a work-life-balanced work environment, but at least people would have (some) enthusiasm and ownership in it. The part I would not be able to–not *dare* to imagine, however, was told in the book: that it was a passionate developers' land of milk and honey, where there was no politics or rushed release dates but creators' freedom and craftsmanship. "There was nothing magic about Blizzard; it was simply one of the only companies in the industry not forced into Faustian bargains with “dumb money” publishers. Because we financed our own games, we could afford to maintain high standards [...] Studios working with publishers rarely have control over their games, especially the shipping dates, which means polishing is never guaranteed. Blizzard didn’t have investors, marketing people, or other non-gamers dictating what to make or when to ship it, or even how it should look. There were no suits. Everyone in the company played games, from the CEO down to our receptionist." It felt like reading a diary written by someone who claimed they had been to El Dorado, which no one believed because the place described was too wonderful to exist. But it did exist, for I, among millions of others, have witnessed the proof, and, unfortunately, the signs showing that it does not anymore.

A recurring theme of Blizzard's games' narratives is the heroes’ downfall, the corruption of the once noble and righteous. In Diablo II, Tal Rasha, the leader of the Horadric Order, also one of the most powerful mages in the world, after learning that the vessel used for imprisoning the prime evil the order had been hunting was broken in the action and thus could not harness the evil forever, volunteered to provide his mortal body as the eternal durance. As the vessel was stabbed into his body, which he had asked the order to chain to the ground, he fought the unbearable pain while wrestling with the demon in his mind, alone, forgotten, forever. Centuries later, when the player was finally able to meet him, Tal Rasha was no more, and the once powerful mage's flesh had become the demon's new shell. In WarCraft III, Arthas, a young prince and an aspiring paladin, encountered a plague which turned his people into undead, village by village. After he found that, while the disease was still latent, a whole city was infected and there was no cure, he alongside his most loyal troop slaughtered the entire city in tears. Vowing vengeance, he chased the demon who spread the epidemic to the end of the world. There he found a sword that would grant him the power to defeat the culprit and a never-ending whisper, which eventually drove him to insanity. He became a loyal servant and the flesh host of the mastermind behind the plague, killed his father, and destroyed his own kingdom before turning it into a scourge that threats the entire planet. After the players defeated him, the soul of his father–the first innocent victim of his cursed sword–incarnated before him. "Father! Is it...over?" "At long last. No king rules forever, my son." This was the finale of the Arthas saga, the most iconic storyline of WoW that started in WarCraft III (2002) and ended in WoW's second expansion, _Wrath of the Lich King_ (2008). The same year Blizzard's parent company merged with the huge game publisher Activision, forming _Activision Blizzard_, which in its nearly two-decade history had many commercially successful titles along with controversies and scandals. After the success of _Overwatch_, the 2016 award-winning multiplayer hero shooter, also their only original title in the last decade, Blizzard announced _Overwatch 2_ in 2019, featuring a story-driven PvE campaign with rich character customization system. Given Overwatch was a game with heavy in-game microtransactions, during the announcement, Jeff Kaplan, the game's lead director and also vice president of Blizzard, stated that the team hoped to "redefine what a sequel means" and "no one gets left behind". Two years later, despite a massively marketed 20-teams esports league having run for three years, which was entirely based on the game's 6v6 design, Blizzard announced that Overwatch 2's team size would be changed to 5v5, causing massive roster and career uncertainties. The game launched in 2022, with Overwatch outright *deleted* from all owners' libraries at the same moment. The promised PvE content was at first delayed until 2023, then largely scrapped from the development plan; what remained was sold as a $15 DLC. 6v6 was added back to the game as a separate queue option. Finally, in February 2026, the "redefined sequel" was renamed to _Overwatch_. Jeff Kaplan, who left Blizzard in 2021, on the renowned podcast [_Lex Fridman Podcast_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H9rF1CSSh-w) revealed the reason that led to his departure: he was called into the CFO's office and was told "Overwatch has to make (undisclosed amount) in 2020 (this date was later slipped to 2021) and a recurring revenue of (undisclosed amount) every year after that. If the task is not achieved, we are going to lay off a thousand people, and that's going to be on you." Making the original game inaccessible on the successor's release is a trick players hate but Blizzard loves. Announced in 2018, _WarCraft III: Reforged_ is the remake of the 2002 original game, featuring fully upgraded visuals, four-plus hours of remade cinematic-style cutscenes, an upgraded World Editor, crossplay with classic version, etc. Almost none of the above promises were kept: new models were delivered but UI stayed the same; cutscenes were in exactly the same style with the two-decade old version except minor camera angle changes; World Editor was updated with new APIs and tools, along with an update to the term of service claiming the ownership of all player-made custom maps; crossplay technically happened, at the price of every owner of WarCraft III, reforged or not, being forced to upgrade to the new client, which had several online features cut, and of the original game removed from libraries, no longer accessible after almost twenty years. Four days after the release, WarCraft III: Reforged received a 0.5 out of 10 user score on Metacritic, which at one point was the lowest across all games. Blizzard afterward granted refund, no questions asked. The chaos and disorder of the organization were shown outside the products as well. Breastfeeding employees alleged that their pumped breastmilk, labeled with babies' face, was stolen from the breakroom fridge. Such incidents can be traced back to 2008. In 2019 _Hearthstone_ player Ng "blitzchung" Wai Chung shouted a Hong Kong protest slogan[^2] in a post-match interview during the live broadcast of an official online tournament. The next day Blizzard cited a ruling against "making politics statement, or damaging Blizzard's image". He was denied all prize money from the tournament in addition to being banned from Hearthstone esports for 12 months, which, after harsh backlash from the community, was cut down to a six months ban and all prize money restored. A photo taken in 2013 showed several male Blizzard employees gathering in a hotel room, hoisting a portrait of comedian Bill Cosby, who was at the time accused of drugging and sexually assaulting women. The poster, a veteran WoW developer, on his personal Facebook page, posted other pictures such as a minibar stocked with plenty of alcohol as well as soft drinks with a caption referring to the room as "Cosby Suite", and a screenshot of a chatroom, in which another man typed "I am gathering the hot chixx for the Coz". Below that message, fellow veteran Blizzard designer Jesse McCree, the namesake of an Overwatch hero, replied "You misspelled fuck". After the photos were reported by Kotaku in 2021 and the individuals in the photo identified, McCree the developer left Blizzard, and McCree the hero was renamed.

The next time I touched WoW was three years after vanilla, in Wrath of the Lich King. I played Wrath like I was possessed. This time it wasn't about the world but the endgame content, that is, raids. There were layers of entertainment in raids: the first was beating the bosses, the interaction between players and the game. My most memorable Wrath raid run was an unfinished one: a bunch of people gathered for the first time, unorganized yet nevertheless playing around each other, and somehow embarked on the raid _Ulduar_, until we faced _Hodir_, a titan who wields the power of winter. Periodically he unleashes a cold spell, freezing anyone who does not escape to the top of small snow bumps in time. Every time a player dies in the battle Hodir yells: “To come so far, only to fail.” It has been almost twenty years; I have never forgotten this line since that night. It takes a group of members who understand the task and their roles, a decisive leader, and endurance for failing over and over; I was in a regular group when we beat _Sindragosa_, one of the most difficult bosses of the expansion. We had known each other from a private chat channel for more than a year, and had been running as a regular 10 people raid team for months. The moment she died for the first time, after tens of wipes, a teammate typed "我就说这把会过[^3]" (I knew we had it this pull). I have played a lot of multiplayer games since, but I never had that feeling again. The second layer was the competition of performance, the interaction between players. I loved the competition of damage per second done to bosses in the weekly raids, to the point I looked forward to the encounters themselves more than the loot. In order to perform well, a vast amount of gold–the in-game currency–was necessary since I was in a semi-private GDKP group, in which instead of relying on dice rolls (i.e. pure luck) to decide who got the loot, the items were auctioned. I would do all the repeatable quests to make money until I hit the daily limit, then I would change character and go again, while chatting with my friends in Azeroth. Perhaps it would seem unfathomable to see someone enthusiastically working in a virtual world to make virtual money, but at the time, I was living in the world. In the story there was, in the middle of the crusade against the undead army, located just before the Lich King's citadel, a friendly tournament between two antagonistic factions, aiming to cool down the growing tension. People were saying that it felt out of place and that the content was filler and repetitive. Objectively they weren't wrong, but when I sat just outside the tournament arena, seeing champions of the two factions doing friendly sport instead of raising weapons against each other, listening to my absolute favorite music in the entire game, I felt a sense of belonging, serenity, and happiness. I would do anything to live that moment again.

My playtime of WoW dropped severely after the _Mists of Pandaria_ (2012) expansion then has been zero since _Shadowlands_ (2020). I still occasionally follow the news about the current game (_retail_, as the community calls it), sometimes about the _Road to World First_ raid races, sometimes about the story, but never about the gameplay. Modern WoW is technically a lot *smoother* to play than the old versions, as in the gameplay friction, such as traversing the world or finding party member through chat, which may disrupt one's experience of a piece of content, along with the social element and serenity that came with it, are largely optimized out. The key metric of the game became *player engagement*, and the purpose of every bit of gameplay was to increase players' time spent in the game. That being said, occasionally I still get hit by a sudden urge to have a taste of it. most of the time I just wait until it passes; sometimes I would play the free trial and be reminded that it wasn't vanilla or Wrath anymore. The world is now a theme park, trying its best to please the customer and at every opportunity, to reduce friction in order to increase retention. The itches then drove me to look for ways to revisit the golden era. I wasn't alone. While the retail servers keep progressing forward, which means old expansions as they were become inaccessible, private servers, which mostly run vanilla or other old expansions, gained popularity; some even achieved thousands of concurrent online players. At the BlizzCon 2013 WoW panel Q&A session, an attendee asked if the development team had considered to add servers that run early expansions. J. Allen Brack, then WoW's executive producer, answered: "No, and you don't want to do that either. You think you do, but you don't." Four years later, at the same event, J. Allen Brack spoke to the crowd: "Fans of World of Warcraft around the world...we hear you. I'm pleased, and also a little nervous, to announce the development of a classic server option for World of Warcraft." As hypocritical as he is, I search for and rewatch this announcement every couple of weeks, and every time I get goosebumps when he mentions vanilla. Needless to say I played Classic the day it launched. Classic was, as Blizzard promised, authentic to how it was back in the day, that is, the content part, which was exactly what the community requested, but ironically worked against itself. A game twenty years old, and an extremely popular one at that, was solved and optimized to the finest detail one could imagine; a key factor of the vanilla magic was the unknown, and in Classic, everything was known. The friction was still there, but the amazement of seeing the world for the first time had long gone. This drastically changed how the players behaved, as when the best routes were found, proved and, worst of all, expected, most of the world became irrelevant. People couldn't bear spending hours wiping in dungeons anymore, and honestly, I couldn't blame them, as those who were teenagers or young adults now have partner, children, or at least a tomorrow on which they have to clock in at 9 AM. Just as I was approaching level 30–the highest level I had reached in the beta–and thought that I was finally entering uncharted territory, the blitzchung incident happened. I quit immediately. I also canceled my Reforged preorder (in hindsight, I probably would have requested a refund anyway), writing the slogan blitzchung yelled in the customer message field; at that time, I really thought I was not going to give Activision Blizzard another dime ever. That continued until I gave in to the itch, despite knowing this wasn't going anywhere, I returned, because Classic is my guilty pleasure, the toxic ex I can never get over. Then I quit again. Then I returned, again, at the moment I heard the news of Classic _The Burning Crusade_, the expansion before Wrath. It wasn't about TBC–I never played that expansion–it was the message that, if Blizzard would do Classic TBC, there was no way they wouldn't do Classic Wrath, the expansion that achieved an all-time high of _12 million_ subscribers. The mere notion of being able to experience Wrath again weighted more than my conscience and what I said about blitzchung. The game was as beautiful as the day I lost it, I had months of good time, then I found myself having had enough of the Classic min-max culture, that finding a raid team was a greater hustle than the raid itself. This obsession with the past, the addiction to nostalgia have become an industry standard with more and more successful past titles being remade and re-released. Following Classic and Reforged was _Diablo II: Resurrected_, the remaster of the 2000 classic and its expansion. It was, unlike Reforged, more authentic to the original with no major gameplay updates but several quality-of-life improvements. Also, unlike Reforged, it was a success. I played (yes, giving money to Blizzard yet again) and immensely enjoyed Resurrected, this time _properly_ progressing through the endgame; this time checking builds, magic find locations and runewords recipes not from paper magazines but websites; this time without a buddy to clear runs for me. Despite successfully making me willing to give them money again, and despite the sobering realization of the return of the game does not equal to the return of the good old days, however, Blizzard still could not regain my trust in their flagship products. I played _Diablo IV_ (2023). Many times I was astonished by its insanely beautiful aesthetics, yet it was, at the end of the day, a theme park I'm sure I won't visit ever again. I haven't had the slightest urge to touch Hearthstone or Overwatch in a decade although both are free to play; they aren't tempting enough to beat my hypocritical conscience. It has to be something that scratches the itch of the refined, selected moments beautified by time. At 2026 BlizzCon, the rumored and anticipated _WoW Forever_ was announced: an expansion-scaled new server staying true to Classic's roots, fixing some decades-long issues, improving the decades-old design; brand-new content, but not in the theme park style; the once denied appeal, now in spotlight. It had an announcement panel, a deep dive panel introducing the major features and changes, a technical panel talking about better ambience powered by enhanced visual, and the technical improvements behind them, each 30 to 50 minutes. I watched them all. It's the game I've looked forward to the most since Classic Wrath–imagine the ex having fixed *that habit* you can't stand. At this point as long as it's about the 2000s classics and sells the promise of visiting the beautiful memory (and does not fail miserably like Reforged) I would buy anything. At the same event they also announced _Diablo V_, merely three years after the release of D4, which was supposed to be a live service game with years or even more than a decade of life span. This time only a photo-realistic cinematic trailer was shown, no gameplay, no "mobile" mentioned. Following the announcement panel was a deep dive panel with a host and five developers, no Q&A, no boos. D2 was mentioned multiple times during the panel, referred to as an inspiration for D5's design, such as the player camp or itemization philosophy. They also did a "Diablo IV: What's next" panel the day after. I didn't watch it.

[^2]: "光復香港，時代革命" (Liberate Hong Kong, revolution of our time)
